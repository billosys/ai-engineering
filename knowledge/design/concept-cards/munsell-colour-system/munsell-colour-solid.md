# Quick Definition

The Munsell solid diagrams hue around a neutral value axis and chroma radially. Historical sphere and tree illustrations serve different explanatory purposes; neither proves automatic balance or exact perceptual geometry.

# Core Definition

The earlier extraction attributes the following wording to Cleland; the book and prepared text are unavailable, so quotations, locators, context and historical attribution remain unverified.

Retained reported wording: "a globe, the north pole of which represents white, the south pole black, and the axis made up of a sequence of grays extending from white to black. Around the equator is a band of hues whose value is 5/ and chroma /5" "in the actual measurement of pigment colors... all of the paths of Chroma would not be of the same length nor would they all be comprised within a sphere. Certain of them would extend to points outside of it" "a 'Color Tree' with a vertical trunk for the scale of Value and branches representing the different Hues, these branches varying in length with the Chroma Strength of each Hue" "The sphere is limited in size to this shortest axis" "all opposite colors will balance because being all of equal length at each level of Value no Chroma path can be longer than another or outbalance it"

The reported sphere/tree illustrations organize colour relations. In a Euclidean illustration constant radial extent is cylindrical; a sphere boundary has r²+(z−z0)²=R². Reference/material gamut can be irregular. Symmetry alone proves neither neutral mixing nor aesthetic balance.

Further recorded extraction wording (unverified attribution/context; these are not adopted universal claims):

- "Colors differ by nature in their Chroma Strength" (p. 8)
- "All colors do not reach their maximum Chroma Strength at the same level of Value" (p. 8)
- "limited only by the strength of pigments" (p. 9)

# Key Properties

1. Angular hue, vertical value and radial chroma define a cylindrical diagram convention.
2. A sphere is a particular Euclidean boundary with radius changing by height; constant radial extent at every height would instead be cylindrical.
3. A practical colour tree depicts chroma extent varying by hue/value and reference/material conditions.
4. Recorded Red /10 and BG /5 pigment comparisons are historical examples, not all-time maxima.
5. An abstract coordinate range does not imply unlimited realizable surface colours or one gamut for all media.
6. Reported rotating-disc demonstrations concern temporal averaging under particular conditions; no such demonstration was performed here.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:

1. Choose a value axis, hue ordering and radial chroma scale.
2. For a Euclidean sphere illustration use r²+(z−z0)²=R² at the boundary.
3. For an empirical tree use an identified set of allowable H/V/C values and conditions, with r_max varying by hue/value.
4. Label any historical sphere's balance claim as a claim to test, not a geometric consequence.

## To Identify/Recognise:

1. Recognize the three diagram dimensions and an identified boundary.
2. At z=z0 a sphere has radius R; at z=z0+R its radius is zero. A constant-r side is cylindrical.
3. An irregular tree or illustrative sphere alone supplies no colour-distance metric.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Inferring automatic harmony or balance from a sphere.
  **Correction**: A symmetric drawing establishes geometry, not perception or mixing. Do not substitute it for measurement or rendered review.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Equal radii at all heights describe a sphere.
  **Clarification**: That describes cylindrical extent; a sphere's boundary radius varies with height.
- **Confusion**: A historic pigment tree supplies all current display/material maxima.
  **Clarification**: Use reference-specific data. An expanded material set changes the sampled gamut, not a universal guarantee of limitless chroma.
