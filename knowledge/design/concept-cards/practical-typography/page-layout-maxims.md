# Quick Definition

Butterick's nine page-layout maxims offer a method for balance and consistency. They are contextual heuristics for composing a document, not necessary conditions or a complete test of design quality.

# Core Definition

Butterick asks the designer to consider the whole page, with these nine principles:

1. **Decide first how the body text will look.** In prose-dominated work, font, point size, line length and leading strongly influence the page. Other projects may begin from genuine content, accessibility, interaction or production constraints.
2. **Divide the page into foreground and background.** Identify the more important roles and avoid incidental elements competing with them; position, size, font and sometimes colour are his tools for distinction.
3. **Make adjustments with the smallest visible increments.** Compare fine changes where useful; no fixed increment is required.
4. **When in doubt, try it both ways.** His appeal to visual samples complements reasoning and explicit constraints; it is not a claim that logic is bad.
5. **Be consistent.** Similar treatments can communicate similar roles, while meaningful differences can distinguish roles and hierarchy.
6. **Relate each new element to existing elements.** His jigsaw-puzzle analogy explains how choices constrain one another; a grid can help.
7. **Keep it simple.** He challenges three colours/five fonts, a logo on every page and useless edge clutter. These are examples for reconsideration, not numerical bans or reasons to discard complex content, accessibility or expression.
8. **Imitate what you like.** Study typography in books, signs and websites to understand useful choices, then adapt to the actual task.
9. **Don't fear white space.** Compose outward from text rather than enlarging elements just to fill the sheet; empty space is available for deliberate relationships.

# Prerequisites

- **Good Typography** — The maxims are possible ways of serving text and context in Butterick's utilitarian account.
- **Body Text** — His first maxim starts with running prose; it is not the only valid design sequence.

# Key Properties

1. **Body-first option**: Useful where prose dominates.
2. **Role hierarchy**: Foreground/background distinctions depend on the content's priorities.
3. **Fine comparison**: Small visible changes can clarify a choice.
4. **Visual judgment**: Samples inform judgment alongside semantics, constraints and testing.
5. **Consistency**: Repeated treatment can expose structure without forcing identical appearance everywhere.
6. **Related choices**: A new element changes the composition around it.
7. **Economy**: Reduce unnecessary choices while accommodating actual complexity.
8. **Learning from examples**: Study what works and why in its context.
9. **Useful white space**: Space within and between groups can carry relationships.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the document's meaning, content roles and genuine constraints.
2. For prose-dominated work, compare body font, size, leading and measure together.
3. Establish foreground/background relationships with deliberate visible distinctions.
4. Compare candidate arrangements, including small optical changes, against constraints and actual output.
5. Coordinate repeated elements and grid references where useful; keep justified differences.

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. Inspect whether visual distinctions reflect the actual hierarchy and task.
2. A feeling of clutter or disorder is a prompt to inspect relationships, not a diagnosis that a design violates a font-count or grid rule.

# Examples

**Example 1** (maxims-of-page-layout): Maxim #5 connects consistent treatment of similar elements to perceived structure and hierarchy. A heading and its body may differ visibly because their roles differ, while headings at a shared level may use a repeatable treatment.

**Example 2** (maxims-of-page-layout): Maxim #9 warns against filling the sheet merely because space is available. Margins, paragraph gaps and interior layout space can be deliberate; the maxim does not require empty space at the expense of content.

# Relationships

## Builds Upon
- **Good Typography** — These methods aim to reinforce text meaning; they are not a universal quality definition.
- **Body Text** — Running prose explains the source's body-first option, not a prerequisite priority for all layouts.

## Related
- **Typographic Grids** — Shared alignment references can support maxims #5 and #6 without guaranteeing quality.
- **Page Margins** — Margins are one location for white space; paragraph and interior spacing also matter.
- **Typographic Color** — Apparent text density and literal colour may affect role contrast; neither substitutes for semantic hierarchy or actual colour-pair review.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Applying body-first order, limited font counts or simplicity as a ban on a justified design.
  **Correction**: Use the maxims as questions within the task's constraints. Preserve meaningful complexity and accessible/expressive solutions.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Optical judgment makes explicit reasoning or technical constraints unnecessary.
  **Clarification**: Visual comparison complements content structure, constraints and actual usability checks. A grid or ratio is an optional aid, not a sufficient quality test.
