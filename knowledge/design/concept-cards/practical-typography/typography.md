# Quick Definition

Typography is the visual treatment of written text: the choices that shape how the same words appear in print, on screen, or in another visual medium.

# Core Definition

Butterick defines typography as “the visual component of the written word” and distinguishes text content from its rendering. The distinction is useful: words can remain the same while typeface, size, spacing, colour, and arrangement change. His account gives utilitarian purpose a central role, while also acknowledging that aesthetic judgment and expressive work are part of typography. It is his position, not a survey establishing that typography has no artistic dimension.

# Key Properties

1. **Visual treatment**: Typography concerns the appearance of text, not the words themselves.
2. **Several controls**: Font choice is one part of typography; size, weight, spacing, colour, and arrangement also contribute.
3. **Purpose and expression**: Choices can support a task or meaning and can also carry aesthetic or expressive intent.
4. **Context matters**: Highway signage and wedding invitations have different audiences and tasks; the examples illustrate different priorities, not a universal legibility result.
5. **Learnable judgment**: Butterick emphasizes teachable skills and purpose-led decisions; this does not make taste irrelevant.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. Compare the same text in two renderings; differences in its visual treatment are typographic differences.
2. Name the relevant properties rather than attributing every difference to font choice.
3. Ask what the text is for, who will read it, and what expressive intent the design carries.

# Context & Application

Typography applies wherever text is visually rendered. In prose-dominated work, give body text early attention because it occupies much of the reading experience; signs, interfaces, labels, and expressive work may prioritize other content or tasks.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating typography as font selection alone.
  **Correction**: Consider size, spacing, weight, colour, and arrangement as well as the font.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Typography must be either utilitarian or artistic.
  **Clarification**: Butterick argues for a strong utilitarian role and dissents from “invisible typography” as a universal doctrine. This records his view; it does not establish a historical consensus or exclude expressive practice.
