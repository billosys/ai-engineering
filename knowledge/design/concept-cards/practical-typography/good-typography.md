# Quick Definition

Good typography is typography that serves the text's purpose and meaning for its intended readers; several treatments may work, and aesthetic success can matter alongside utility.

# Core Definition

Butterick's central thesis is that good typography reinforces the meaning of the text. He argues that context and goals matter more than an abstract ranking and that multiple solutions can work for the same text. His stronger claim that an aesthetically unpleasant result can succeed expresses his utilitarian framework; it is not a rule that aesthetics never matters.

# Prerequisites

- **Typography** — The distinction between text and its visual treatment supplies the subject being evaluated.

# Key Properties

1. **Purpose-led**: Ask whether the treatment supports the text's task, meaning, and audience.
2. **Context-dependent**: A choice that works for one text or setting may not suit another.
3. **Multiple workable solutions**: Typography is not an equation with one correct visual answer.
4. **Utility and aesthetics**: Utility is central in Butterick's framework, but aesthetic and expressive goals may also be part of the brief.
5. **Evidence-bounded judgment**: A sign example illustrates a design rationale; it does not by itself establish universal legibility or tested user outcomes.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. State the intended task, readers, and constraints.
2. Check whether hierarchy and visual treatment support that task.
3. Compare plausible alternatives in the actual context; do not infer success from a style label alone.

# Context & Application

Use this as a critique method for documents, interfaces, signage, and other work. The relative priority of body text depends on the artifact: it is a sensible early focus in prose-dominated work, while a sign or interface may prioritize a different task.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Judging work only by whether it looks “clean” or conforms to one style.
  **Correction**: Relate the judgment to purpose, readers, constraints, and any aesthetic goals in the brief.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Either typography must be invisible, or utility makes appearance irrelevant.
  **Clarification**: Butterick rejects invisible typography as a universal rule and gives utility a central role. Neither point proves a historical consensus or removes aesthetic judgment from other contexts.
