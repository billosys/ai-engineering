# Quick Definition

Typewriter habits is Butterick's label for 22 practices he criticizes in prose typesetting. Use the checklist to question unexamined defaults and substitutions in context; it is not an exhaustive error taxonomy or proof that each listed value is inherently bad.

# Core Definition

The prepared appendix collects 22 practices Butterick associates with the typewriter era. His broader account links typographic compromises to mechanical limitations, but the checklist itself does not independently establish the historical origin or frequency of every practice.

Its useful design question is whether a default or approximation serves this text, language and medium. Monospace for code, underlined links, meaningful capitals, a justified 12pt choice, one-inch margins, or deliberate feature settings can be appropriate. Review actual content, font capabilities and house style rather than removing them by category.

# Prerequisites

- **Typography** — Distinguishing written content from its visual treatment supplies the subject for evaluating these choices; it does not establish a ranking of error causes.

# Key Properties

1. **Source checklist**: There are 22 numbered items, not a comprehensive classification of typographic error.
2. **Historical framing**: Butterick associates them with typewriters; this is attribution, not a newly verified history.
3. **Default versus decision**: An unexamined preset differs from a deliberately selected value that fits the task.
4. **Connected controls**: Character choice, point size, leading and measure interact with actual content; checklist membership is not proof that a particular output fails.
5. **Useful starting cases**: Items #1–3 concern prose quotes, sentence spaces and dash approximations; #17/#21/#22 question relying exclusively on size/leading/margin defaults.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify/Recognise:
Butterick's 22-item list, paraphrased in its original order, criticizes:

1. Straight prose quotes instead of curly quotes.
2. Two sentence spaces instead of one.
3. Multiple hyphens instead of dashes.
4. Alphabetic trademark/copyright approximations.
5. Three periods instead of an ellipsis character.
6. Non-curly apostrophes.
7. Omitting accented characters.
8. Multiple word spaces.
9. Tabs/tab stops instead of tables.
10. Carriage returns used for vertical space.
11. Alphabetic substitutes for mathematical symbols.
12. Repeated characters used as rules/borders.
13. Ignoring ligatures.
14. Underlining.
15. Monospaced instead of proportional fonts.
16. Abusing all caps.
17. Assuming 12 is the best body-text point size.
18. Ignoring kerning.
19. Ignoring letterspacing.
20. Too much centered text.
21. Only single/double line spacing.
22. Only the line length permitted by one-inch margins.

Treat these as his contextual criticisms. Preserve literal code/data and required transcription; respect real accents and symbols. Underlining may identify links, monospace may express code, and caps may belong to actual content. Ligatures, kerning and tracking depend on the selected font/script and task. A 12pt size, a spacing preset or one-inch margins needs contextual judgment, not automatic rejection.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Reviewing prose typography for unexamined defaults or inappropriate substitutions, regardless of how the writer learned to type.
- **Common applications**: A starting checklist tied to content goals and actual output; eliminating listed features alone does not guarantee good typography.
- **Historical note**: Butterick mentions Robin Williams's The Mac is Not a Typewriter and its PC sequel as earlier popular treatments. This is his bibliographic mention, not an independently researched origin or priority claim.

# Examples

**Example 1** (typewriter-habits): The source's first three items connect straight prose quotes, two sentence spaces and repeated hyphens to his criticism. Compare prose “hello”—an aside with code `print("hello")` or literal `--help`; the latter retain their syntax. His rhetoric elsewhere about typewriters never typesetting professional publications is not independent historical evidence.

**Example 2** (typewriter-habits): Item #17 criticizes assuming the best body size is 12, rather than every actual 12pt setting. Read it with #21 (only single/double leading) and #22 (only the measure from one-inch margins): choose size, leading and measure together for the text. No fixed alternative guarantees hierarchy or readability.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating every checklist item as a universal prohibition, or removing meaningful content to satisfy it.
  **Correction**: Evaluate the intended prose style and actual output; preserve code, data, links, house style and supported script/font behavior.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: A familiar habit must be right, or a typewriter-associated value must always be wrong.
  **Clarification**: Evaluate whether the choice serves the text and task. Neither personal teaching nor Butterick's historical rhetoric settles the result; contextual judgment remains necessary.
