# Quick Definition

The New Typography advocates standardization, especially DIN paper formats, as an economic and aesthetic response to format "chaos." Shared sizes can support production, storage and use when those workflows need them; standards do not guarantee quality, lower prices or universal superiority.

# Core Definition

This section preserves the card’s recorded account of Tschichold’s historical programme; quotations, attributions and page claims have not been checked against the unavailable represented edition. Its polemics are not universal contemporary rules.

Tschichold presents standardization as integral to modern typography: "The New Typography, in its concern to satisfy the needs of our own period and to make sure that every single piece of printing is in harmony with the present, has always taken the greatest interest in every move towards standardization."

The argument is both practical and philosophical: "The need for standardization, in whatever area, derives from the problems of today, which it aims to solve." The economic case: "standardization is a guarantee for the raising of quality and lowering of prices."

He traces the history from Lichtenberg (1796) through the French Revolution to Dr. Porstmann and the DIN system, presenting standardization as a rational culmination of format reform; this origin narrative remains reported and unverified.

# Key Properties

The historical preferences below describe the programme; current use depends on content, audience, language/script, output, constraints and supplied identity.

1. **Potential economy**: Shared formats may reduce production, stock and filing costs; evaluate actual workflow and trade-offs rather than assuming savings.
2. **Aesthetic advantage**: Standard formats "give good page proportions" — their artistic possibilities "are unlimited."
3. **Separate design choices**: Paper size, letterhead layout, margins, columns and type areas interact, but a format standard does not determine all of them or define a responsive interface.
4. **Against arbitrary formats**: "It is usually a sign of incompetence when a non-standard format is insisted on 'for artistic reasons.'"
5. **Benefits all parties**: Users (simpler filing), printers (efficient production), manufacturers (fewer sizes), and tradesman (simplified stock).

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify production, content, filing, storage and distribution constraints before selecting a format.
2. Use a compatible standard where it helps; justify custom dimensions where they better serve content, identity or output. No paper standard mandates screen widths, breakpoints or spacing tokens.
3. Consider the item's place in a system — how will it be filed, stored, mailed alongside other standard items?

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. Standardized design: items in uniform sizes that file and store together logically.
2. Custom formats can help or hinder actual production and use; variation alone is not proof of waste.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Paper sizes, document formats, business forms, publication layout.
- **Common applications**: ISO 216 paper sizes (A4, A5, etc.), design systems with standardized component sizes, responsive design breakpoints.
- **Historical note (reported, unverified here)**: The DIN 476 paper standard was adopted in Germany in the 1920s and became the international ISO 216 standard, used worldwide except in North America. The principle of standardization extended from typography to become foundational in design systems thinking.

## Cross-Domain Connections

**Engineering → STRUCTURAL**: DIN paper standardization applies the same industrial standardization logic used in engineering — replacing arbitrary individual choices with rational systems. The editorial parallel concerns coordinated constraints and compatibility, not proof that standards always improve cost/quality or that one paper ratio defines every design system.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating any existing standard or custom format as automatically correct for the task, including its "artistic" purposes.
  **Correction**: The historical polemic says "It is usually a sign of incompetence when a non-standard format is insisted on 'for artistic reasons.'" Contemporary judgment compares production, content, identity and expression; a custom format can be justified.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Standardization constrains creativity.
  **Clarification**: "The artistic possibilities of standard formats... are unlimited." A chosen standard can simplify decisions, but constraints can also exclude useful alternatives; compare actual task benefits and costs.
