# Quick Definition

Tschichold’s anti-ornament programme calls for eliminating decorative rules, printers' flowers, "abstract decorations," and even the thick/thin rule, invoking "a fear of pure appearance." This is a historical stance, including oppressive cultural rhetoric, not a contemporary ban on cultural or non-modernist ornament.

# Core Definition

This section preserves the card’s recorded account of Tschichold’s historical programme; quotations, attributions and page claims have not been checked against the unavailable represented edition. Its polemics are not universal contemporary rules.

The card reports Tschichold citing architect Adolf Loos (1898; attribution/date unverified here): "The more primitive a people, the more extravagantly they use ornament and decoration... To seek beauty in form itself rather than make it dependent on ornament should be the aim of all mankind."

The recorded argument applies that culturally hierarchical rhetoric to typography: "The use of ornament, in whatever style or quality, comes from an attitude of childish naivety. It shows a reluctance to use 'pure design,' a giving-in to a primitive instinct to decorate — which reveals, in the last resort, a fear of pure appearance. It is so easy to employ ornament to cover up bad design!"

The programme’s scope of "ornament" is broader than expected: "'Ornament' is not only decorated rules and printers' flowers but also includes all combinations of rules. Even the thick/thin rule is an ornament, and must be avoided." Also: "'Abstract decorations' which some foundries have produced under different names are also ornaments in this sense."

# Prerequisites

- **Functional Typography** — The programme applies its interpretation of function to ornament. Contemporary decoration can serve cultural, affective and identity purposes, so rejection is not a necessary consequence of function.

# Key Properties

The historical preferences below describe the programme; current use depends on content, audience, language/script, output, constraints and supplied identity.

1. **Broad definition**: Ornament includes decorated rules, flowers, thick/thin rules, "abstract decorations," and geometric shapes used without function.
2. **Covers old and new**: Both traditional floral ornaments and "elementary ornaments" (circles, triangles used decoratively) are rejected.
3. **Culturally hierarchical rhetoric**: The quoted claims about primitive peoples and childishness are oppressive historical judgments, not valid contemporary diagnoses of people or their design traditions.
4. **Historical criticism**: Ornament may obscure a weak composition, but its presence alone does not establish failure; assess the actual purpose and effect.
5. **Not mere omission**: Simply removing ornament (as in the "Das politische Buch" letterhead) is not enough — functional form must replace it.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. When studying the programme, compare its omission strategy with the actual composition. For contemporary work, identify cultural, emotional and identity purposes before removing ornament.
2. Compare emphasis through contrast, spacing and weight; preserve decoration that supports the actual cultural, affective or identity purpose.
3. The programme asks geometric shapes to emphasize, group or guide. Contemporary shapes may also serve cultural expression; inspect their role and effect.
4. A removal comparison can reveal effects on grouping, expression and identity; no change in literal reading alone proves an element purposeless.

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. The programme classifies certain appearance-oriented marks as ornament. That classification does not prove they lack cultural or communicative purpose.
2. Functional success requires assessing actual content, expression, people and use; geometry or omission alone is insufficient.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Studying the modernist ornament critique and deciding whether particular elements help the actual brief.
- **Common applications**: Modern flat design, minimal UI and content-first web work can use omission as one approach; their labels do not establish an anti-ornament obligation.
- **Historical note (reported, unverified here)**: Loos published "Ornament und Verbrechen" (Ornament and Crime) in 1908. Tschichold applies the argument specifically to typography in 1928. The principle was moderated over the century — postmodernism reintroduced ornament, and current practice acknowledges decorative elements can serve emotional and cultural functions.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Replacing traditional ornaments (flowers, borders) with "modern" ornaments (circles, bold rules, triangles).
  **Correction**: "If these are merely substituted for the old ornaments, nothing is improved." That is the programme’s critique, not a blanket ban on purposeful contemporary ornament.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Ornament rejection means all visual elements besides type must go.
  **Clarification**: Rules, shapes, and colour are legitimate when they serve a communicative function — emphasis, contrast, grouping, direction. This explains the historical programme’s allowance for such marks; contemporary cultural, affective or identity decoration is not thereby banned.
