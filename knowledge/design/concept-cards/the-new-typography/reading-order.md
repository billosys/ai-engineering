# Quick Definition

Reading order distinguishes visual entry and emphasis from continuous-text direction, meaningful programmatic sequence and focus order. Tschichold argues for successive visual steps — "it is shown at its clearest that we read most printed matter in successive steps." Prominence can invite entry without predicting every reader’s eye path.

# Core Definition

This section preserves the card’s recorded account of Tschichold’s historical programme; quotations, attributions and page claims have not been checked against the unavailable represented edition. Its polemics are not universal contemporary rules.

Tschichold distinguishes between the conventional reading direction and actual reading behaviour: "The typographer must take the greatest care to study how his work is read and ought to be read. It is true that we usually read from top left to bottom right — but this is not a law."

He proposes an account of how readers encounter printed matter: "It is shown at its clearest in Willi Baumeister's invitation card. There is no doubt that we read most printed matter in successive steps: first the heading (which need not be the opening word) and then, if we continue to read the printed matter at all, we read the rest bit by bit according to its importance."

The practical implication: "It is therefore quite feasible to start reading a text at a different point from the top left. The exact place depends entirely on the kind of printed matter and the text itself." But with a caution: "one must therefore, in general, not set a following body of text higher than the preceding one."

# Key Properties

The historical preferences below describe the programme; current use depends on content, audience, language/script, output, constraints and supplied identity.

1. **Different sequences**: Visual entry, continuous-text/script direction, DOM/programmatic sequence and focus order are distinct. Departure from a page’s usual visual entry does not abolish text direction or semantic dependencies.
2. **Possible scanning**: Emphasis can invite scanning in steps, but readers may enter and continue differently according to task and familiarity.
3. **Proposed entry**: A heading may attract attention and "need not be the opening word." This is not an observed eye-tracking result here.
4. **Content-dependent**: "The exact place depends entirely on the kind of printed matter and the text itself."
5. **General caution**: Following text should generally not be placed higher than preceding text.
6. **Speed of modern reading**: "As a rule we no longer read quietly line by line, but glance quickly over the whole."

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the entry point — what should the reader see first?
2. Design a plausible visual hierarchy while retaining script-appropriate continuous text and meaningful document/programmatic sequence.
3. Use type size, weight, and position to guide the reading sequence.
4. A glance test is one inspection of visual emphasis. Separately check meaningful source order, keyboard focus and the target output; CSS visual reordering cannot repair semantic sequence. A glance is not user or assistive-technology proof.

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. Useful order: intended groups and transitions are understandable; evaluate visual, programmatic and focus sequences rather than assuming a natural universal eye path.
2. Poor reading order: confusion about where to start or how to continue (e.g., the poster with "ten sizes of type" where "it is very difficult to find where to go on reading").

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Forcing the reader to find their way through a layout that prioritises visual form over reading sequence.
  **Correction**: "Typographic form must be organic, it must evolve from the nature of the text" — let content hierarchy inform visual sequence while preserving meaningful source and focus order.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: All text must start at the top left.
  **Clarification**: A visual entry may differ from the start of continuous text. Preserve script direction, meaningful programmatic order and focus order; visual freedom does not waive these obligations.
