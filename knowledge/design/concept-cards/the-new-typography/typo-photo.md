# Quick Definition

Typo-photo is the synthesis of typography and photography into a unified graphic composition. The recorded programme calls it "the collective form of graphic art" replacing "handwriting-drawing"; that preference is historical. Photography may depict depth on a flat surface without creating a third physical dimension.

# Core Definition

This section preserves the card’s recorded account of Tschichold’s historical programme; quotations, attributions and page claims have not been checked against the unavailable represented edition. Its polemics are not universal contemporary rules.

The card records this definition (coinage attribution unverified): "By typo-photo we mean any synthesis between typography and photography. Today we can express ourselves better and more quickly with the help of photography than by the laborious means of speech or writing."

The synthesis has a formal basis: "there is only one objective type form — sanserif — and only one objective representation of our times: photography. Hence typo-photo, as the collective form of graphic art, has today taken over from the individualistic form handwriting-drawing."

The key aesthetic principle is contrast: "At first sight it seems as if the hard black forms of this typeface could not harmonize with the often soft greys of photos. The two together do not have the same weight of colour: their harmony lies in the contrast of form and colour. But both have two things in common: their objectivity and their impersonal form."

He further argues: "Purely flat typography belongs to the past. The introduction of the photographic block has enabled us to use the dynamics of three dimensions."

# Key Properties

The historical preferences below describe the programme; current use depends on content, audience, language/script, output, constraints and supplied identity.

1. **Synthesis, not juxtaposition**: Photography and type form a unified composition.
2. **Contrast-based approach**: Flat type versus represented photographic depth, and hard black versus soft grey, are recorded compositional options; no actual third dimension is introduced.
3. **Recorded objectivity ideal**: The programme calls sans-serif and photography "objective"; selection, framing, editing and cultural interpretation prevent treating that ideal as inherent neutrality.
4. **Photography as type element**: The photographic block "joins letters and rules in the compositor's case as a contemporary but differentiated typographic element."
5. **Represented depth**: Photographs can depict spatial depth on the flat page; the actual physical composition remains two-dimensional.
6. **Historical pairing preference**: "the problem of type never actually existed: it had to be sanserif." Contemporary photography can work with serif or sans-serif type; neither category guarantees objectivity.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the role of the image and type in the actual brief; deliberate integration need not give both equal visual priority.
2. Compare flat type and represented photographic depth as one compositional approach; the image may also be deliberately flat.
3. Choose supplied serif or sans-serif faces appropriate to script, content and identity; inspect framing and editing rather than claiming maximum objectivity.
4. Use colour only where it helps the actual roles; preserve the supplied palette and check rendered pairs/states and non-colour cues. Red is not a default.

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. Typo-photo: photography and type composed as a unified visual whole.
2. Old approach: photos treated as insertions into a text layout, separate from the typographic composition.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Advertising, editorial design, posters, book covers.
- **Common applications**: Modern editorial design, web hero sections combining photography and typography, social media graphics.
- **Historical note (reported, unverified here)**: The concept was developed simultaneously by Moholy-Nagy (who coined "typophoto" in 1925) and Tschichold. It became the dominant paradigm for advertising and editorial design throughout the 20th century.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating photographs as decorative additions to an otherwise complete typographic layout.
  **Correction**: The photograph must be an integrated compositional element, not a tacked-on illustration.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Sans-serif type and photographs cannot harmonise because of their different visual weights.
  **Clarification**: The recorded reply is "Their harmony lies in the contrast of form and colour. But both have two things in common: their objectivity and their impersonal form." Contrast is one useful compositional option; objectivity is the programme’s ideal rather than an inherent property of type or photographs.
