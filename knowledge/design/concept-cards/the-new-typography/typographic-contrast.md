# Quick Definition

In Tschichold’s programme, typographic contrast — the deliberate use of contrasting visual properties (large/small, light/dark, horizontal/vertical, coloured/plain) — is the primary means of creating visual hierarchy and meaning in functional typography, with the recorded preference that "large differences in weight are better than small." Actual clarity depends on meaning, pairs, state, font rendering and context.

# Core Definition

This section preserves the card’s recorded account of Tschichold’s historical programme; quotations, attributions and page claims have not been checked against the unavailable represented edition. Its polemics are not universal contemporary rules.

Tschichold elevates contrast to a fundamental design principle: "The real meaning of form is made clearer by its opposite. We would not recognize day as day if night did not exist. The ways to achieve contrast are endless; the simplest are large/small, light/dark, horizontal/vertical, square/round, smooth/rough, closed/open, coloured/plain; all offer many possibilities of effective design."

His practical guidance is emphatic: "Large differences in weight are better than small. The closer in size different types are to each other, the weaker will be the result." And: "A limit to the number of type sizes used — normally three to not more than five — is always to be recommended."

The principle serves function: "Every part of a text relates to every other part by a definite, logical relationship of emphasis and value, predetermined by content. It is up to the typographer to express this relationship clearly and visibly, through type sizes and weight, arrangement of lines, use of colour, photography, etc."

# Key Properties

The historical preferences below describe the programme; current use depends on content, audience, language/script, output, constraints and supplied identity.

1. **Contrast supports distinctions**: Differences can expose intended relationships; meaning also depends on content, conventions and context.
2. **Seven contrast pairs**: large/small, light/dark, horizontal/vertical, square/round, smooth/rough, closed/open, coloured/plain.
3. **Historical preference for large differences**: "The closer in size different types are to each other, the weaker will be the result."
4. **Scoped size recommendation**: The recorded three-to-five suggestion is neither a global maximum nor a required minimum. The poster’s six-size remark concerns a specific example.
5. **Logical, not arbitrary**: "All form must correspond with meaning and not contradict it."
6. **Recorded black-white-red example**: "The already strong contrast between black and white can be greatly enhanced by the addition of red." This is not a mandatory palette; actual foreground/background pairs and states require checking.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. Identify the logical hierarchy of the content (what is most important, what is secondary).
2. Compare size, weight, spacing, arrangement and colour for the actual content; a very large headline is an option, not a requirement.
3. Choose the number of levels needed; three-to-five is attributed advice, not a universal ratio or count. Size alone cannot express every difference in emphasis, grouping or state.
4. Reinforce hierarchy with weight, colour, and spatial positioning.
5. Verify: does every contrast correspond to a meaningful distinction in the content?

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. Strong contrast: clear visual hierarchy, immediate identification of importance levels.
2. Weak contrast: "everything looks the same" — insufficient differentiation between elements.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Using many type sizes with small differences between them.
  **Correction**: The recorded advice says "A limit to the number of type sizes used — normally three to not more than five — is always to be recommended." Inspect actual hierarchy rather than enforcing that count; weight, spacing and colour can help when size alone is insufficient.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Strong contrast means visual chaos.
  **Clarification**: Contrast must be logical — every visual difference must correspond to a meaningful content distinction. Unlimited contrast without logic produces "pseudo-constructivism."
