# Quick Definition

Tschichold argues that sans-serif ("Grotesque" or "block letter") is "the only one in spiritual accordance with our time" — its plainness, clarity, and geometric construction express the same values as modern architecture, making it a requirement within his recorded programme, not for all contemporary typography.

# Core Definition

This section preserves the card’s recorded account of Tschichold’s historical programme; quotations, attributions and page claims have not been checked against the unavailable represented edition. Its polemics are not universal contemporary rules.

Tschichold's argument is absolute: "None of the typefaces to whose basic form some kind of ornament has been added (serifs in roman type, lozenge shapes and curlicues in fraktur) meet our requirements for clarity and purity. Among all the types that are available, the so-called 'Grotesque' (sanserif) or 'block letter' ('skeleton letters' would be a better name) is the only one in spiritual accordance with our time."

He links typeface to architecture: "To proclaim sanserif as the typeface of our time is not a question of being fashionable, it really does express the same tendencies to be seen in our architecture."

Yet he acknowledges imperfection: "There is no doubt that the sanserif types available today are not yet wholly satisfactory as all-purpose faces." He praises Paul Renner's Futura as "a significant step in the right direction" while noting that "all the attempts up to now... are merely 'improvements' on the previous sanserifs: they are all still too artistic, too artificial."

For body text: "The main reason why sanserif is so seldom used today for normal text setting is that in general there is not enough of it available." When sans-serif is unavailable, he recommends "a good roman, and sanserif will be reserved for emphasis."

# Key Properties

The historical preferences below describe the programme; current use depends on content, audience, language/script, output, constraints and supplied identity.

1. **Historical exclusivity claim**: The programme treats sans-serif alone as meeting its clarity/purity ideal; this is not a universal legibility or fitness result.
2. **Attributed classification**: The programme classifies serifs as ornament. A serif face can serve contemporary reading; serif/sans describes letterform features, not a quality ranking.
3. **Recorded architecture parallel**: The programme associates sans-serif with the spirit of modern architecture, rather than establishing an intrinsic association for every audience.
4. **Not yet perfected**: Available sans-serifs are "not yet wholly satisfactory" — the ideal typeface will come from a group, including an engineer.
5. **Ranking**: (1) Jobbing sans-serifs, (2) Venus and copies (worse), (3) "painterly" block letters (third choice).
6. **Recorded roman fallback**: The programme recommends plain romans (Sorbonne, Nordische Antiqua) when sans-serif is unavailable and rejects decorative or historical faces; contemporary selection retains contextual alternatives.
7. **Reported book choice**: The card reports a sans-serif setting intended to demonstrate readability; the unavailable represented edition prevents a current whole-book or empirical conclusion.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:
1. Read the sans-serif mandate as historical advocacy. For contemporary work, start with the actual language, script, content, reading duration and supplied identity.
2. The programme’s plain, "uninteresting" roman fallback is a recorded preference; inspect candidate serif or sans-serif families for glyphs, shaping, styles and real text.
3. Check loading, licensing, fallbacks, size, spacing and rendered hierarchy in the intended output; no family category guarantees readability.
4. Its rejection of "artists' typefaces," historical faces, script and decoration is not a contemporary ban; choose them when appropriate to the brief.

## To Identify/Recognise:
1. The New Typography in practice: sans-serif type, often in multiple weights for hierarchy.
2. The programme’s old-typography classification includes decorated types, fraktur and ornamental romans; this is not a present-day quality verdict.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Historical typeface advocacy and contextual contemporary comparison of serif/sans candidates.
- **Common applications**: Sans-serif selection in interfaces, branding and signage where the actual script, identity and reading task support it.
- **Historical note (reported, unverified here)**: Tschichold's advocacy was enormously influential. Sans-serif became the default for modernist design and later for digital interfaces. He specifically praised Futura (1927), which remains in wide use. His later career saw him designing in traditional roman for book typography (Sabon, 1967), effectively contradicting his 1928 absolutism.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Treating historical condemnation of a type category as a present-day fitness test.
  **Correction**: The polemic says "It is absurd today to build villas like Rococo palaces or Gothic castles, so people tomorrow will smile at those who continue to practise the old typography." Assess actual content, culture and output rather than importing that condemnation.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: Sans-serif is advocated because it is fashionable or novel.
