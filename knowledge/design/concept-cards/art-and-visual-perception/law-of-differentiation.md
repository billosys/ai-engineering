# Quick Definition

In Arnheim's represented developmental account, the law of differentiation holds that in the development of visual representation, forms begin undifferentiated — a single global shape standing for a whole category — and progressively specialise through the emergence of specific distinctions that mark differences among kinds and parts.

# Core Definition

This section presents the represented Arnheim account. Its quotations, page references and historical, clinical, cultural or experimental reports have not been checked against the unavailable original. The current analytic qualifications do not certify that fidelity.

Arnheim proposes this law as a principle of individual (child art) and cultural (primitive art) development, using historical terminology: "sizes will not be differentiated unless there are good reasons for it" — and more broadly, no distinction will be introduced until cognitive development permits and requires it.

The represented account gives these cases:
- A circle first stands for *all* compact objects (head, trunk, planet, flower, animal).
- Straight lines first stand for *all* elongated shapes, regardless of organic or geometric character — before "rigidity" and "curvature" are differentiated as distinct visual qualities.
- The right angle first stands for *all* angular relations — obliqueness is not differentiated as a distinct category until a later stage.
- Size is undifferentiated until there is a cognitive reason to make things different sizes.

The account invites interpreting nonrealistic drawing by its purpose rather than assuming error. It does not establish that all learners, cultures or styles pass through one necessary first stage. It cautions against misreading "simplicity" as "rigidity," "undifferentiation" as "emotional meaning."

Arnheim quotes Wölfflin: "All effects are relative. The same form does not mean the same thing at all times. The meaning of the vertical in Renaissance portraits differs from that in the portraits of the primitives. Here it is the only form of representation; there it is set off from other possibilities and thus acquires its particular expression."

# Prerequisites

- **Perceptual simplicity** — Provides the represented preference for regular or undifferentiated forms, not proof of a universal starting state.

# Key Properties

1. **Reported global/local sequence**: Arnheim describes broad distinctions preceding fine ones; this is not a universal curriculum.
2. **Reported simplicity**: Right angles and circles figure in the historical model, not a mandatory design order.
3. **Context-relative expression**: A straight line can serve different roles where other choices are available.
4. **Attributed sequence**: "An artist does not skip steps." (Cocteau, cited by Arnheim) is preserved as a reported maxim, not evidence of an exceptionless law.
5. **Subdivision and integration**: The account describes both adding distinctions and combining parts.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Identify/Recognise:

1. Ask which distinctions are present and which needed facts are omitted.
2. Inspect whether hierarchy makes roles clear at the actual sizes and styles.
3. Compare category and subtype distinctions in an icon set without assuming one teaching sequence.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Visual development in children's art; the history of art styles; design system construction; visual literacy education.
- **Common applications**: Designing typographic scales (coordinate sizes and weights with content roles); building icon systems (make needed category and subtype distinctions clear); discussing a student's intended distinctions without diagnosing maturity or personality from the result.

# Examples

The following are reported source examples and interpretations, with their original wording and locators retained for inspection. They are not independently checked historical, experimental or universal claims.

**Example 1** (p. 201): "Straight lines look stiff in comparison with curved ones. For this reason adults... sometimes misread the straight legs, arms, or fingers of early drawings as 'rigid' and proceed to use them diagnostically as symptoms of a rigid personality." This misreads undifferentiated straight-line representation as specific expression of rigidity.

**Example 2** (p. 204): Arnheim reports right-angle copying before oblique copying in the Stanford-Binet comparison: average five-year-olds copy a square and average seven-year-olds a diamond. This historical test account is unassessed and does not establish universal ages or a necessary teaching sequence.

**Example 3** (p. 289): Sizes in early pictorial representation are not differentiated unless there is positive reason for differentiation. A fox and crow in the Aesop's Fables illustration are drawn the same size because they are narrative equals — not because the child doesn't know their real size difference.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Interpreting undifferentiated features in early art or student work as errors, defects, or emotional symptoms.
  **Correction**: An omitted distinction may be deliberate, unneeded or a task error; none establishes personality, incompetence or a universal stage.

- **Error**: Trying to teach advanced differentiation (central perspective, complex foreshortening) before basic ones are mastered.
  **Correction**: Choose instruction from the learner's needs and actual difficulties. The represented developmental account does not prove that every advanced technique must wait for the same sequence.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: "Differentiation" means "making things look more realistic."
  **Clarification**: Differentiation means introducing the distinctions that carry meaning *for the visual system and purpose*. Greater realism is one outcome, but differentiation includes non-representational distinctions like size hierarchy, directional contrast, and weight variation.
