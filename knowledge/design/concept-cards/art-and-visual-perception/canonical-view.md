# Quick Definition

A canonical view is a candidate aspect that makes an object's discriminating structure recognizable for a particular task and audience. This card applies that later term to Arnheim's discussion of the "best" aspect; no single angle is optimal for every use.

# Core Definition

This section presents the represented Arnheim account. Its quotations, page references and historical, clinical, cultural or experimental reports have not been checked against the unavailable original. The current analytic qualifications do not certify that fidelity.

Because a 3D object can be projected from infinitely many angles, and no single 2D projection can capture all structural properties, the question arises: which view to choose?

The represented discussion weighs two criteria for a "best" view:
1. **Simplicity**: Among all projections, those that produce the simplest, most regular patterns are perceptually dominant. "The dominant projections are those which produce patterns of the simplest shape." (p. 75)
2. **Structural adequacy**: The chosen view should make the structural skeleton of the visual concept directly visible. The visible structure should serve the selected recognition or explanation task; a feature useful for one task may hide another.

These criteria sometimes conflict. A straight front view of a horse (Figure 88) is the simplest projection but is inadequate because its structural skeleton (a compact, penguin-like outline) does not reflect the horse's actual structure. A side view is more complex as a projection but structurally more adequate. Similarly, a top-down view of a Mexican in a large hat (Figure 82) is a perfectly valid orthogonal projection but communicates nothing distinguishing about the subject — the projection's skeleton (a disk) misleads.

For objects with multiple parts whose best views differ, a compromise view, several separate views or an Egyptian composite can each be appropriate.

# Prerequisites

- **Picture plane projection** — Canonical view is about which projection to select.
- **Structural skeleton** — A view can be informative when its axes and visible features serve the selected task.

# Key Properties

1. **Candidate simplicity**: Regularity is one criterion, which can conflict with informative features.
2. **Task adequacy**: Identify the symmetry, proportions or features the actual audience needs.
3. **Part conflicts**: Different aspects reveal different parts; a compromise or composite view can serve the purpose.
4. **Reported history**: The child and "primitive" art comparisons are historical claims using period vocabulary, not a universal skill or cultural ladder.
5. **Convention and expression**: Familiarity can help recognition; atypical aspects can also explain, document or express the subject.

# Construction / Recognition

## To Construct/Create:

1. Identify the subject and the task: recognition, measurement, assembly or expression.
2. List the distinguishing features and any exact appearance required.
3. Compare aspects for feature visibility, simplicity and audience familiarity.
4. For conflicting features, consider the Egyptian composite method, multiple views or a compromise; a composite need not describe one camera.

## To Identify/Recognise:

1. Ask which needed features this aspect reveals or hides.
2. Check whether an atypical aspect serves a deliberate task.
3. Compare icons with their audience and use context; a uniform angle can be appropriate when recognition is retained.

# Context & Application

- **Typical contexts**: Icon and pictogram design; product photography; information illustration; UX design (menu icons, toolbar buttons); scientific illustration.
- **Common applications**: Selecting the right view for an icon (a telephone icon is usually shown at an angle, not straight-on; a coffee mug from a slightly elevated side view); product photography choices; medical illustration.

# Examples

The following are reported source examples and interpretations, with their original wording and locators retained for inspection. They are not independently checked historical, experimental or universal claims.

**Example 1** (p. 75, Fig. 82): A top-down view of a Mexican in a large sombrero is geometrically correct (an orthogonal projection) but is inadequate as a canonical view — the structure is indistinguishable from a millstone or a doughnut. The view fails the structural adequacy criterion.

**Example 2** (p. 75): A bull's typical shape is conveyed by a side view, which however hides the characteristic lyre pattern of the horns. The wingspread of a flying duck does not show in profile. Each part has a different canonical view.

**Example 3** (p. 76): The Egyptian method resolves the canonical-view problem by combining the most informative aspect of each part: front view of the eye in a profile head; front view of the chest with side view of the body. Each part is shown in its canonical view, even at the cost of projective consistency.

# Common Errors

- **Error**: Assuming the most photographically natural or "realistic" view is canonical.
  **Correction**: Compare structural adequacy, simplicity, photographic convention and the intended task. The most "natural" angle may or may not reveal the needed features.

- **Error**: Designing all icons at the same angle (e.g., all at 45° isometric).
  **Correction**: Useful aspects depend on the object's features, the task and the audience. A uniform angle across all icons may serve stylistic consistency but can sacrifice structural legibility for some objects.

# Common Confusions

- **Confusion**: "Canonical view" sounds like it means a standard or conventional angle.
  **Clarification**: Here the term describes an informative aspect relative to a task. Familiar conventions can influence recognition; this card does not establish that all conventions originate in a single perceptual preference.
